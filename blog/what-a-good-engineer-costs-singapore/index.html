What a good engineer actually costs in Singapore right now

The salary is the part everyone sees — it isn’t the whole cost. An honest breakdown of what an engineering hire really costs, good or bad, and how to budget for it.

Eugene Teo

Co-Founder, JTE Recruit · 22 September 2026 · 4 min read

The short version: a good engineer’s salary is only the visible part of the cost. Add employer CPF, leave and benefits (roughly another 15–25% on top), the cost of hiring itself, and the ramp-up time before they’re fully productive — and the real number is meaningfully higher than the figure on the offer letter. The most expensive line of all, though, is a bad hire, or a critical role left open. Here’s the honest full picture, and how to budget for it.

When employers ask us “what does a good engineer cost?”, they usually mean the salary. That’s the number everyone anchors on — and it’s the one that gets a role stuck when it’s set too low. But budgeting on salary alone is how good hires get away and bad ones get expensive.

Start with the salary — but treat it as a range

Engineering salaries vary widely by discipline, industry and seniority — a junior maintenance engineer and a senior process or controls engineer are worlds apart, and the same title pays differently across semiconductor, oil & gas, construction and consumer products. Rather than a single figure, anchor to a current range for the specific role. Our engineering salary guide keeps live ranges by role and level, so you can pitch the offer where it needs to be to actually land someone.

The on-costs everyone forgets

The salary isn’t what the hire costs you. On top sits employer CPF (up to 17% for most employees), annual leave and public holidays, any bonus, insurance and benefits, and the smaller statutory bits. As a rule of thumb, budget the true employment cost at roughly 1.15 to 1.25 times the base salary — more if your package is generous. It’s not a reason to hesitate; it’s just the number you should actually be planning against.

The cost of hiring itself

Filling the role costs money before the person even starts. There’s the time your team spends writing the spec, screening and interviewing; the agency fee if you use one (typically a percentage of annual salary for a permanent placement — more on agency costs here); and the quiet, easy-to-ignore cost of the role sitting open — work not done, a team stretched, a project slipping. A role open for three months has already cost you something, whether or not it shows up on an invoice.

The expensive one: a bad hire

Every cost above is predictable. The one that isn’t — and the one that dwarfs the rest — is getting the hire wrong. A mis-hire in a technical role can mean months of salary paid, work that has to be redone, a team’s momentum lost, and then the full cost of hiring again from scratch. For a critical engineering role, the true cost of a bad hire is routinely a multiple of the salary. That’s exactly why the money is better spent getting the right person than saving a few thousand on the wrong one.

Where underpaying quietly costs more

It’s tempting to treat the lowest salary as the cheapest option. In engineering it often isn’t. Underpay and you widen the field to people who couldn’t get more elsewhere, you lose good hires to the next offer, and you pay in turnover, mistakes and rework. The classic version is the “last drawn” trap — anchoring an offer to what someone earned before, rather than what the role is worth — which quietly filters out exactly the people you wanted. Paying right for the work is usually the cheaper path in the end.

How to budget properly

Budget the total cost of the hire, not the salary: base × roughly 1.2 for on-costs, plus the cost of hiring, plus a few months of ramp-up before full productivity. Then decide where flexibility helps — for a finite project or an uncertain need, a contract or contract-to-perm arrangement lets you access the skill without the full permanent commitment, and lets you confirm the fit before you lock it in.

Frequently asked questions

How much does a good engineer cost in Singapore?

It varies widely by discipline, industry and seniority, so there’s no single figure — anchor to a current range for the specific role rather than a headline number. And remember the salary isn’t the full cost: employer CPF, leave and benefits typically add another 15–25% on top. Our engineering salary guide keeps live ranges by role and level.

What is the employer’s CPF contribution rate?

For most employees the employer contributes up to 17% of wages to CPF, with lower rates for older age bands. It’s one of the main on-costs to budget on top of the base salary.

How much does it cost to hire an engineer through an agency?

For a permanent placement, agencies typically charge a percentage of the hire’s annual salary. Contract staffing is priced differently — as a margin on the contractor’s rate. We break the models down in our guide to recruitment agency costs.

Is it cheaper to hire a cheaper engineer?

Usually not, over time. Underpaying tends to cost more through turnover, mistakes and rework, and it filters out the stronger candidates who can command more elsewhere. Paying appropriately for the work is generally the cheaper path.

What does a bad engineering hire cost?

For a critical technical role, often a multiple of the salary — months of pay, work that must be redone, lost team momentum, and the full cost of hiring again. It’s the single biggest reason to invest in getting the right person rather than the cheapest one.

How should I budget for an engineering hire?

Budget the total cost, not just the salary: base pay times roughly 1.2 for on-costs, plus the cost of hiring, plus a few months of ramp-up before full productivity. For finite or uncertain needs, contract or contract-to-perm can reduce the commitment.
