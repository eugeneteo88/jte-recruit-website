How to make a job offer they won’t reject

You found the right person, made the offer… and they said no. Here’s how to stop that happening — because most rejected offers are avoidable.

Eugene Teo

Co-Founder, JTE Recruit · Employer guide · 7 min read

Short answer: most rejected offers aren’t about the money — they’re about how the offer was made. Too slow, too low against an expectation you never checked, no proper sell, and a long silent gap between “we want you” and the actual letter where a counter-offer or a rival swoops in. As an employer, few things sting more than doing all the interviewing, finally finding the right person, and then watching them say no. The good news: nail the basics of the offer stage and that rejection becomes rare. Here’s the playbook.

We manage this exact moment for clients all the time, and I can tell you the offers that fall over almost always trip on the same handful of avoidable things. Let’s go through them.

Why good candidates actually reject offers

It’s tempting to assume a rejection means “we didn’t pay enough”. Sometimes true — but just as often it’s something else: the offer came too slowly and they’d already committed elsewhere; the number was a surprise because nobody checked their expectations; the offer landed as a cold email with no warmth or sell; or their current boss threw a counter-offer at them and nobody had prepared them for it. Every one of those is fixable, and none of them is really about your budget.

Know their expectations before you make the offer

The number one cause of a rejected offer is a surprise. You should never be guessing what someone expects on salary, notice period, or whether they’re sitting on other offers — you should have asked, gently, well before offer stage. If their expectation is way above your range, you want to know that at interview two, not after you’ve fallen in love with them and drawn up a letter. No surprises, on either side: that’s the whole game. A good recruiter does a lot of this expectation-managing quietly in the background, which is half of what a good recruiter actually does.

Speed saves offers — slowness kills them

Once you know you want someone, the clock is your enemy. Good candidates are often kiasu — scared to lose out — so if there’s a long, quiet gap between the final interview and the offer, they’ll assume you’re not serious, or another company will simply move faster. I’ve seen perfect hires lost purely because the employer took two weeks to get internal sign-off while a competitor closed in three days. Get your approvals sorted before the final interview, so the moment you decide, you can move.

Make an offer, not a lowball

Anchoring your offer to someone’s last-drawn salary is a very Singapore habit, and it’s a trap. What they earned before tells you what their last employer paid, not what this role is worth or what it’ll take to move them. Offer to the value of the role and the market (our salary guide and cost breakdown help you pitch it right). A cheeky lowball to “test the water” mostly just insults a good candidate and pushes them toward the offer that respected them.

Sell the offer — don’t just send it

An offer isn’t a form, it’s the close of a sale — and the person on the other side has options. Pick up the phone before the letter goes out. Tell them you’re genuinely excited to have them, why they stood out, and what they’ll get to do and learn. That two-minute call does more than the fanciest offer letter, because it makes them feel wanted rather than processed. People join teams that clearly want them.

Prepare them for the counter-offer they’ll get

Here’s the one employers forget. The day your candidate resigns, their current boss will very likely panic and counter — more money, promises of change, a bit of guilt-tripping. If you haven’t talked about that possibility in advance, you’ve left them to face it alone, and plenty get talked into staying. Have the honest conversation early: “when you resign, they may counter — here’s why people who accept usually leave within a year anyway.” (We wrote the whole case in should you counter-offer — from the other side of the desk.) Forewarned, they hold firm.

Don’t forget the little things

Small stuff closes offers too. A bit of flexibility on the start date so they can finish well with their current employer. A warm welcome note, or a quick call from the team they’ll join. Clear answers on the practical bits — leave, benefits, first-day logistics — so nothing feels vague or shaky. None of it costs much, and together it tells a candidate this is a place that has its act together and actually cares. That reassurance is often the final nudge.

Use more than money to sweeten it

If your salary number is already at the top of what you can do, remember money isn’t your only lever — and often not the deciding one. Flexibility on hours or a day from home, a clear growth path, a better title, more interesting projects, a real learning budget: these matter enormously to good people, and they cost you far less than another few hundred dollars a month. When you can’t win on the number, win on the things the bigger, stiffer companies can’t easily offer. It’s one of the genuine advantages of being a smaller, more human employer — so use it.

Keep them warm between offer and start date

An accepted offer is not a done deal. The gap between signing and starting is where hires quietly slip away — especially if they’re serving a long notice and their current boss is working on them daily. Don’t go silent for two months. A friendly check-in, an invite to a team lunch, sending over anything they need before day one — small touches that keep them feeling wanted and connected. The candidate who already feels part of the team before they start is the one who actually turns up on day one.

When they still say no

Sometimes you do everything right and they still decline — a better fit elsewhere, a personal reason, cold feet. Don’t take it personally and don’t burn the bridge. Ask, genuinely, what tipped the decision — the honest answer is gold for your next offer — and leave the door open warmly. Candidates talk to each other, and the employer who took a “no” with grace is the one who gets recommended to their friend, or comes back a year later when the other thing didn’t work out.

Frequently asked questions

Why do candidates reject job offers?

Usually not just money. Common reasons: the offer came too slowly and they committed elsewhere, the salary was a surprise because expectations weren’t checked, the offer was a cold email with no sell, or their current employer counter-offered and they weren’t prepared for it. Most of these are avoidable at the offer stage.

Should I make a verbal offer first?

Yes — a warm phone call before the written offer closes far more candidates than a letter alone. It lets you show genuine enthusiasm, explain why they stood out, and answer any hesitation on the spot, so the written offer just confirms a decision they’ve already felt good about.

How fast should I make an offer after the final interview?

As fast as you can — ideally within a day or two. Good candidates move quickly and often have other options, so a long silent gap loses them. Get internal approvals sorted before the final interview so you can act the moment you decide.

How do I stop a candidate accepting a counter-offer from their current employer?

Prepare them for it in advance. Tell them, before they resign, that a counter-offer is likely and why people who accept one usually leave within a year anyway. A candidate who expects the counter and understands the trap is far more likely to hold firm.

Should I base my offer on the candidate’s last-drawn salary?

No. Last-drawn tells you what their previous employer paid, not what the role is worth or what it’ll take to move them. Offer to the value of the role and the current market; anchoring to last-drawn often lowballs good people and pushes them toward a competitor who didn’t.

What should I do if my job offer gets rejected?

Don’t burn the bridge. Ask genuinely what tipped the decision — the answer will sharpen your next offer — and leave the door open warmly. Candidates talk, and handling a “no” gracefully often leads to a referral or the same person returning later.

Getting the offer stage right — managing expectations, moving fast, and prepping candidates for the counter — is a big part of what we do for clients, and it’s often the difference between a hire and a near-miss.
