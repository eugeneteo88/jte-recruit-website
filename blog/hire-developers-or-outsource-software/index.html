Hire developers, or outsource the software? (build vs buy for SMEs)

Bring it in-house, or hand it to a vendor? Here’s how to make the call — and the hidden costs nobody mentions on either side.

Eugene Teo

Co-Founder, JTE Recruit · 24 November 2026 · 6 min read

Short answer: hire in-house when software is becoming core to how you run and you’ll keep needing to change it; outsource when it’s a one-off build or you need to move fast without a permanent team. Most SMEs actually do both at different stages. The trap is committing to one on reflex — hiring a permanent developer for a one-off project, or outsourcing something so central to the business that you can’t afford to lose control of it. As an employer who’s built software both ways, here’s how I’d think it through.

It’s one of the biggest early decisions a non-tech business makes, and getting it wrong is expensive — so it’s worth slowing down on.

The real question: core and ongoing, or one-off?

Strip away the jargon and it comes down to two things. Is this software going to be core to how your business runs — the thing customers touch, the system you’ll keep improving — or is it a one-off you’ll build once and mostly leave alone? And will you need to keep changing it, or set it and forget it? Core and ever-changing points to in-house; one-off and stable points to outsourcing. Answer those honestly and the decision mostly makes itself.

When outsourcing makes sense

Outsourcing is often the right first move: you get speed and a whole team’s worth of skills without hiring anyone permanently, which is ideal for a one-off build, a first version to test an idea, or a specialist piece you don’t need forever. It’s also the sensible choice when you have nobody in-house who could manage a developer — a good vendor comes with its own management. For getting something built and out the door, it’s hard to beat.

The hidden costs of outsourcing

But go in with eyes open, because the cheap-looking option has real costs. The knowledge of how your system works lives with the vendor, not you — and it walks out the door if you part ways. Every change becomes a quote and a wait, which gets slow and pricey once you’re iterating. You can end up locked in, dependent on one supplier who knows where everything is. And their incentives aren’t always yours — a vendor is done when the build is delivered; you have to live with it for years. None of this means don’t outsource; it means price it in.

When to bring it in-house

Once software is genuinely core and you’re changing it constantly, in-house usually wins. You get speed of iteration (no quote for every tweak), the knowledge stays in the building, and you have someone who actually cares about the thing long-term rather than moving on to the next client. If your product is the software, or the system is central to how you make money, you want that capability under your own roof — and that starts with getting the first hire right, which is its own minefield (see your first tech hire).

The hybrid most SMEs land on

In practice, plenty of businesses do both in sequence: outsource to build the first version fast, then hire in-house to own and evolve it once it’s proven and central. That gets you to market quickly without a premature permanent hire, and brings control home once it matters. The key is planning the handover — make sure the code, the documentation and the knowledge actually transfer, rather than staying locked in the vendor’s head.

The first-hire trap to avoid

One warning either way: don’t hire a junior developer whose main job is to “manage” an outsourced build they can’t actually judge. That’s the worst of both worlds — you’re paying for oversight that can’t really oversee. If you’re bringing it in-house, hire someone senior enough to own it, or get an outside technical read while you build up. More on that judgement call in how to tell if a developer is good.

Frequently asked questions

Should I hire developers or outsource the software?

Hire in-house when software is core to your business and you’ll keep changing it; outsource for a one-off build, a first version to test an idea, or when you have nobody to manage developers. Many SMEs outsource to build, then hire to own and evolve it once it’s proven.

Is it cheaper to outsource software development?

It can look cheaper upfront, but factor the hidden costs: knowledge stays with the vendor, every change becomes a paid quote, you can get locked in, and their incentives end at delivery. For a one-off it’s often genuinely cheaper; for something core you’ll keep changing, in-house usually wins over time.

When should I bring software development in-house?

When the software is core to how you run, you’re iterating on it constantly, and you want the knowledge and control in the building rather than with a supplier. That’s the point where the speed and ownership of an in-house team outweigh the flexibility of outsourcing.

Can I outsource first and hire later?

Yes, and it’s a common, sensible path — outsource to build the first version quickly, then hire in-house to own and evolve it once it’s proven and central. The key is planning the handover so the code, documentation and knowledge actually transfer to your team.

How do I keep control if I outsource?

Insist on owning the code and the intellectual property, get proper documentation, and avoid being wholly dependent on one supplier who alone knows how everything works. Build in a real handover from the start, so you’re never held hostage by a vendor who holds all the knowledge.
