Hiring a nurse in Singapore? Why it takes longer than you think

The problem employers keep hitting: you find a nurse, want them to start next month — and then discover it doesn’t work that way. Here’s why, and how to plan around it.

Eugene Teo

Co-Founder, JTE Recruit · 8 December 2026 · 7 min read

Here’s a problem I watch healthcare employers walk into again and again: they find a good nurse, want them to start next month, and then discover it simply doesn’t work that way. Every nurse in Singapore has to be registered (as a Registered Nurse) or enrolled (as an Enrolled Nurse) with the Singapore Nursing Board before they can practise — and for a foreign-trained nurse, that’s a real, multi-month process, not a formality. This isn’t a lecture on the rules — it’s about the timeline problem that catches employers out, why it exists, and how to plan around it so you’re not left staring at a gap you can’t fill. (Requirements are set by the SNB and do change, so always confirm the current position.)

I’ll keep this practical and employer-focused — not a legal treatise, just what tends to trip people up when they hire nurses.

RN vs EN: know which one the role needs

There are two categories, and they’re not interchangeable. A Registered Nurse (RN) has the broader scope of practice and more clinical autonomy. An Enrolled Nurse (EN) provides nursing care under the direction and supervision of RNs, with a narrower scope. When you write the role and set the pay, be clear which one you actually need — advertising for an RN when the work is really an EN role (or vice versa) muddies your search and your budget. The registration category has to match the job.

The rule: no registration, no practice

You cannot simply hire someone with a nursing background and put them on the floor. To practise nursing in Singapore, they must hold valid SNB registration or enrolment — and checking that is your responsibility as the employer, not an optional nicety. Registration also has to be maintained, so it’s not a one-and-done at hiring. For any nurse you take on, confirm their SNB status is current.

Hiring a locally-trained nurse

The simpler path: graduates of nursing programmes accredited by the SNB (the local diploma and degree routes) apply to the SNB for registration or enrolment. For employers, this is the smoother hire — the person is already in the local system — which is one reason locally-trained nurses are in such demand, and why growing your own talent through the local pipeline is worth thinking about.

Hiring a foreign-trained nurse: the real process

This is where employers underestimate the timeline. In broad strokes, a foreign-trained nurse must:

  • Have a job offer first. The SNB generally only considers a foreign nurse’s application once they have an offer of employment from an eligible healthcare institution (those with inpatient services). So the offer has to come before the registration — you’re part of the process, not a bystander to it.
  • Have equivalent qualifications and home registration. Their pre-registration nursing programme must be assessed by the SNB as equivalent to an accredited Singapore programme, and they must hold nursing registration in the country where they trained.
  • Pass the SNB Licensure Examination. Foreign-trained nurses sit and must pass the SNB’s RN or EN Licensure Examination, conducted in English, and meet minimum English-language proficiency.
  • Provide verification. Registration is verified with the nursing authority in their country of first registration (and any country they’ve practised in).
  • Serve a probation. Foreign RNs are typically assessed on the job over a probation period of around six to twelve months before they’re confirmed.

Add it up and it’s months, not weeks. The practical lesson: if you’re hiring foreign-trained nurses, start early and plan the pipeline — don’t expect to fill a gap next month this way.

One catch for clinics without inpatient services

Because the foreign-nurse pathway is generally tied to an offer from an institution with inpatient services, smaller settings without them can find the direct foreign-hire route harder. If that’s you, it’s worth getting advice on your options early rather than assuming you can hire the same way a hospital does. (Always confirm the current rules with the SNB for your specific situation.)

Don’t forget the work pass

SNB registration is the professional licence to practise — it’s separate from the right to work here. A foreign nurse also needs a valid work pass from the Ministry of Manpower, and the two run in parallel. Factor both into your timeline. Our guide to Employment Pass, S Pass and Work Permit covers the work-pass side.

What this means for you as an employer

Three takeaways. First, always verify SNB status — for any nurse, local or foreign. Second, for foreign hires, plan months of lead time and build it into your workforce planning; the manpower crunch makes it tempting to rush, but the process won’t be rushed. Third, because it’s slow and gated, retention of the nurses you already have is doubly valuable — more on that in keeping your care staff.

Frequently asked questions

Do nurses need to be registered to work in Singapore?

Yes. To practise nursing in Singapore, a person must be registered as a Registered Nurse (RN) or enrolled as an Enrolled Nurse (EN) with the Singapore Nursing Board (SNB). It’s a legal requirement, and verifying it is the employer’s responsibility. Always confirm current requirements with the SNB.

What’s the difference between a Registered Nurse (RN) and an Enrolled Nurse (EN)?

A Registered Nurse has the broader scope of practice and more clinical autonomy. An Enrolled Nurse provides nursing care under the direction and supervision of RNs, with a narrower scope. When hiring, match the registration category to what the role actually requires.

Can a foreign-trained nurse work in Singapore?

Yes, but they must register with the SNB first. In broad terms they need a job offer from an eligible institution (with inpatient services), qualifications assessed as equivalent plus home-country registration, a pass in the SNB Licensure Examination (in English) with English proficiency, and verification of their registration — and foreign RNs typically serve a probation. Confirm the current process with the SNB.

Do foreign-trained nurses have to pass an exam?

Yes. Foreign-trained nurses sit and must pass the SNB Registered Nurse or Enrolled Nurse Licensure Examination, which is conducted in English, and they must meet minimum English-language proficiency, as part of registering to practise in Singapore.

Does a foreign nurse need a work pass as well as SNB registration?

Yes — they’re separate. SNB registration is the professional licence to practise; a valid Ministry of Manpower work pass is the right to work here. A foreign nurse needs both, and employers should plan both into the timeline.

How long does it take to hire a foreign-trained nurse?

Plan for months rather than weeks. Between the qualification assessment, the licensure examination, verification, work-pass processing and an on-the-job probation for RNs, it’s a multi-step process — so start early and build the lead time into your workforce planning.
