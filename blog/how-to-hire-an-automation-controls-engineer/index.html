Can’t find an automation engineer who knows your PLC setup? The realistic playbook

Automation is where Singapore manufacturing is investing, so controls engineers are in demand and rarely idle — and brand mismatch quietly kills half your shortlist. Here’s how to actually hire one.

Eugene Teo

Co-Founder, JTE Recruit · 27 October 2026 · 7 min read

Short answer: this is the tightest engineering market in Singapore right now. Everyone’s automating, so good controls engineers are basically never idle — and brand mismatch (Siemens vs Allen-Bradley vs Mitsubishi) quietly kills half your shortlist before you even start. I’ll be honest, this is one of the hardest roles we work. To actually land one: be realistic about brand-match, reach the passive people, hire adjacent and train, or use contract-to-perm to de-risk it. Here’s the playbook.

If you’re building out automation and can’t find a controls engineer who fits, you haven’t done anything wrong — you’ve hit the hardest engineering market to hire in right now. The trick is knowing which levers actually move it.

What an automation & controls engineer does

They design, program and maintain the systems that run machinery: PLCs (the industrial controllers), SCADA and HMI (the software and screens that monitor and control a line), sensors and instrumentation, and increasingly robotics and system integration. They’re the bridge between the physical hardware and the software telling it what to do — which is exactly why they’ve become indispensable as factories automate, and why they’re so contested.

One distinction worth getting straight before you advertise: a controls engineer (PLC, SCADA, instrumentation) is not the same as a robotics or system-integration engineer, even though the roles overlap and job ads blur them constantly. Controls is about the logic and signals that run a line; robotics integration is about specifying, programming and integrating robot cells and automated equipment into that line. Some engineers genuinely do both; many are strong in one and light in the other. If your project is really a robot cell, hiring a pure PLC person (or vice versa) is a mismatch you’ll only discover after they start — so be precise about which you need.

Why it’s the tightest market in engineering

Two squeezes at once. First, demand: automation is where a lot of Singapore manufacturing is investing, so good controls engineers are commissioning lines somewhere, not browsing job ads. Second, brand specificity: someone fluent in Siemens isn’t automatically productive on Allen-Bradley (Rockwell), Mitsubishi or Omron. So your shortlist shrinks twice — few candidates overall, and fewer still on your exact platform.

“You win this market by reaching people who aren’t looking — not by posting an ad and waiting.”

Fix 1 — be realistic about brand-match

This is where most employers quietly sabotage their own search. Insist on an exact brand match and the pool collapses to almost nothing. But a strong controls engineer can cross PLC platforms with a short ramp — the underlying logic, the approach to commissioning, the safety thinking all transfer. Separate the genuine must-haves (say, the platform your critical line runs on) from the nice-to-haves, and you widen the pool without lowering the bar.

Fix 2 — reach the passive pool

More than almost any other engineering role, this one is filled by approaching people who aren’t looking. An advert barely scratches the market, because the engineers you want are busy and employed. Direct, targeted outreach to passive candidates — the core of what a specialist recruiter does — is usually the only route that reliably works here.

Fix 3 — hire adjacent and train

If the perfect match won’t appear, build toward it. An instrumentation or electrical engineer with some PLC exposure can be grown into a full controls role, often faster than you’d expect — and frequently faster than waiting months for a unicorn who never applies. It widens your options dramatically, and a clear development path helps you keep them once they’re trained.

Fix 4 — use contract-to-perm to de-risk

For a scarce, expensive, make-or-break hire, trialling on contract first protects you: you confirm the fit on real work before committing to a permanent seat. It also gets a capable person onto your line sooner while you decide. Our guide to contract vs permanent engineers covers how to structure it, and contract staffing with payroll explains how the arrangement works.

The OT/IT security angle you shouldn’t ignore

As production lines connect to networks, controls engineers increasingly sit on the boundary between operational technology and IT. A candidate with a sense of network segmentation, data flow and basic security around automated equipment is a growing plus — and a genuine differentiator as more factories get connected. It’s worth asking about, even if it’s not your first requirement.

What to check in the interview

  • Platforms: which PLC and SCADA brands have they actually worked on, and how deeply?
  • Commissioned systems: ask them to walk you through one they took from design to running, not just tweaked.
  • Safety: functional safety awareness isn’t optional around automated machinery.
  • OT/IT crossover: any sense of security and networking as lines get connected.

On budget, this is a role where scarcity firmly sets the price — our engineering salary guide and our breakdown of what a good engineer costs will keep you realistic before you go to market.

In-house engineer, or a system integrator?

Before you hire, it’s worth asking whether you need a permanent controls engineer at all, or whether a system integrator should deliver the project. An integrator is often the right call for a one-off build where you don’t have ongoing automation to maintain afterwards. A permanent hire makes sense when automation is becoming central to how you run — you want someone in-house who knows your lines intimately, responds fast when they stop, and keeps improving them over time. Plenty of manufacturers end up with both: an integrator for the big builds, and a permanent engineer to own and evolve the systems day to day. Deciding which you actually need saves you from hiring permanently for what was really a project.

Keeping controls engineers once you’ve hired them

In a market this tight, holding onto the engineer you’ve got is as important as hiring them — they’re exactly the people other employers are trying to poach. Keep them with genuinely interesting work (new lines, new technology, not just firefighting the old ones), exposure to the platforms and tools they want to grow on, and pay that keeps pace with a rising market rather than lagging it. Replacing a controls engineer means re-entering the hardest market in engineering from scratch, so in this field a good retention plan is quietly the cheapest hire you’ll ever make.

Where employers go wrong here

The number one way I see bosses sabotage their own controls search is being too kiasu about the exact brand — “must have five years on our precise PLC model” — which shrinks an already tiny pool down to basically nobody. A strong controls engineer crosses brands with a short ramp; the logic and the safety thinking transfer. Separate what’s genuinely must-have from what’s merely nice-to-have, and suddenly you’ve got people to actually talk to.

The bottom line

You don’t win the automation market by posting an ad and waiting — the people you want aren’t reading it. You win by being smart about brand must-haves, going out to reach the ones who aren’t looking, and being willing to train an adjacent engineer or trial someone on contract first. Play it that way, and even this tight market opens up.

Frequently asked questions

What does an automation or controls engineer do?

They design, program and maintain the systems that run machinery — PLCs, SCADA and HMI, sensors and instrumentation, and increasingly robotics and system integration. They’re the bridge between the physical hardware and the software controlling it, which makes them central to any automation project.

Why are automation and controls engineers so hard to hire?

Two reasons at once: demand is high (automation is where manufacturers are investing, so good engineers are already busy and rarely looking), and the skill is brand-specific, so someone strong on one PLC platform isn’t automatically productive on another. That double squeeze makes it the tightest engineering market to hire in.

Does the PLC brand really matter when hiring?

It matters, but less than most employers assume. A strong controls engineer can cross platforms — Siemens, Allen-Bradley, Mitsubishi, Omron — with a short ramp, because the underlying logic and approach transfer. Insisting on an exact match shrinks your pool needlessly; separate genuine must-haves from nice-to-haves.

Can an electrical or instrumentation engineer move into controls?

Yes, and it’s one of the best ways to fill a role you can’t hire directly. An electrical or instrumentation engineer with some PLC exposure can be developed into a full controls engineer, often faster than waiting for a perfect match to appear on the market.

How much does a controls engineer cost in Singapore?

Scarcity keeps rates firm, so expect to pay for genuine capability. Rather than a single figure, benchmark the specific role and platform against the current market — our engineering salary guide keeps ranges by level, and remember on-costs sit on top of base salary.

Should I hire a controls engineer permanent or on contract?

Permanent suits an ongoing automation function; contract or contract-to-perm suits a specific build or lets you de-risk a scarce, expensive hire by confirming the fit on real work first. Given how tight the market is, contract-to-perm is often the pragmatic route.
