Why your care staff keep leaving — and how to keep them

In healthcare, the cheapest hire is the good person you don’t lose. Here’s what actually drives care staff out — and what holds them.

Eugene Teo

Co-Founder, JTE Recruit · 5 December 2026 · 6 min read

Short answer: in a market this leaky, keeping your good care staff matters as much as hiring new ones — and they rarely leave purely for money. They leave because they’re burnt out, because they feel unseen and undervalued, or because their pay quietly fell behind while the work got heavier. The good news is that most of what holds people is within your control, and cheaper than the constant hiring war. As a recruiter who staffs healthcare, here’s what I see actually work.

Every care worker you keep is one you don’t have to fight the whole world to replace. So retention isn’t a soft topic here — it’s your most cost-effective staffing strategy.

Burnout is the number one driver

Healthcare work is physically and emotionally heavy, and in a short-staffed setting it compounds — fewer hands means more load on the people who stayed, which burns them out, which makes more of them leave. It’s a spiral. The single biggest thing you can do is protect people from an unmanageable, relentless workload: sensible rosters, real breaks, enough cover so one person isn’t carrying three. It sounds obvious, but it’s the difference between a stable team and a revolving door.

Feeling unseen

Care staff often do quiet, hard, unglamorous work that nobody thanks them for. The reliable ones especially — the ones who never kick up a fuss and just get on with it — are the ones employers stop noticing, right up until they resign. Genuine recognition costs almost nothing and holds people more than you’d think: a real thank-you, being asked how they’re doing and actually listening, a supervisor who notices the extra mile. People stay where they feel seen.

Pay that fell behind

Money isn’t usually the first reason care staff leave, but it’s often the last straw — when the work keeps getting heavier and the pay doesn’t move, loyalty wears thin. And in a tight market, they will find out what they’re worth elsewhere. You don’t have to be top of the market, but letting good people fall clearly behind it, while asking more of them, is a bet you’ll lose. Keep pay honest to the market before it becomes a resignation.

No path, no growth

People want to feel they’re going somewhere. A care worker who can see a path — more training, a route to a higher qualification or a senior role, new skills — has a reason to stay that a slightly bigger offer elsewhere doesn’t easily beat. Investing in your people’s development is both a kindness and a retention tool, and in a field crying out for skills, growing your own is smart on every level.

Flexibility, where you can give it

Rostering is the heart of a care worker’s life — it decides whether they can see their kids, sleep properly, have a life. Even a little genuine flexibility and fairness in how shifts are assigned buys enormous goodwill, because it’s the thing that most directly affects whether the job is buay tahan (unbearable) or livable. You can’t remove the shift work, but how humanely you handle it is a real lever.

The stay conversation

Finally, talk to your good people before they resign, not after. Ask what would make them stay, what’s wearing them down, what would make the job better — while you can still act on it. It’s the cheapest retention tool there is and almost nobody in healthcare uses it. And when someone does hand in notice, resist the panic counter-offer reflex; fix the real thing instead. (The same truth as in every field — see why your people keep leaving and should you counter-offer.)

Frequently asked questions

Why do healthcare and care staff leave their jobs?

Rarely just money. The big drivers are burnout from an unmanageable workload, feeling unseen and undervalued, pay that fell behind while the work got heavier, no path to grow, and inflexible rostering. In a short-staffed setting these compound — fewer hands burn out the rest, who then leave too.

How do I retain care staff without a big budget?

Focus on what’s cheap and powerful: protect people from unmanageable workloads, recognise their work genuinely, give some fairness and flexibility in rostering, offer a path to grow, and keep pay honest to the market. Most of what holds care staff costs far less than constantly replacing them.

Is burnout really the main reason care staff quit?

It’s the biggest single driver. Healthcare work is heavy, and short-staffing compounds it — more load on those who stay, which burns them out and drives more departures. Protecting people from a relentless workload with sensible rosters, real breaks and enough cover is the highest-impact retention move.

Does pay matter for keeping care staff?

It’s often the last straw rather than the first reason — but letting good people fall clearly behind the market while asking more of them will eventually lose them, because in a tight market they find out their worth. You don’t have to top the market, just not fall behind it.

What is a stay interview and does it work in healthcare?

It’s a conversation with your good people before they resign — asking what would make them stay and what’s wearing them down, while you can still act on it. It’s one of the cheapest retention tools there is and badly underused in healthcare, where losing and replacing staff is especially costly.
