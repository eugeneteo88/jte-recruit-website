Hiring cybersecurity talent when everyone’s fighting for it

Huge demand, a genuine shortage, and job titles that all blur together. Here’s how to work out who you actually need — and actually land them.

Eugene Teo

Co-Founder, JTE Recruit · 21 November 2026 · 7 min read

Short answer: cybersecurity is one of the hardest hires in Singapore right now — huge demand, a real shortage, and roles that get lumped together confusingly. To actually staff it: work out which security role you genuinely need (they’re not interchangeable), be realistic about certifications versus real ability, reach the passive people who aren’t applying, grow talent from adjacent IT, and use contract or managed help for the gaps. Here’s the playbook, because posting a generic “cybersecurity engineer” ad and waiting is a fast way to fill nothing.

Security is a field where a vague brief hurts more than almost anywhere, because “cybersecurity” covers wildly different jobs. So the first fix isn’t sourcing — it’s clarity.

Why it’s so hard to hire

Two forces at once. Demand has exploded — every business now has something to protect and regulators expect it — while the supply of genuinely experienced security people simply hasn’t kept pace. So the good ones are employed, courted constantly, and rarely reading job ads. If you’re competing on a generic posting alone, you’re fishing in the emptiest part of a very crowded pond.

First, know which security role you actually need

“Cybersecurity” is at least three different jobs, and hiring one when you needed another is a costly mismatch:

  • SOC analyst / defensive — monitors, detects and responds to threats day to day. The watchtower.
  • Penetration tester / offensive — actively tries to break your systems to find the holes before attackers do.
  • GRC / compliance — governance, risk and compliance: policies, audits, standards, regulatory alignment. Often not deeply hands-on-technical, and frequently what a business actually needs first.

Be honest about which problem you’re solving. A lot of employers post for a hands-on hacker when what they really need is someone to get their policies and compliance in order.

Certifications vs real ability

Security is cert-heavy — and certs matter, especially for compliance-facing roles and for getting past HR filters. But a wall of acronyms on a CV isn’t the same as being able to actually secure your systems, and some brilliant practitioners are lighter on paper than the courses suggest. Use certs as one signal, not the whole test. Ask them to walk you through a real incident they handled or a real weakness they found — how they think about risk tells you more than the badges.

Reach the passive people

As with the scarcest engineering roles, the strongest security people are almost never on the job boards — they’re employed and constantly approached. Filling these roles means going out to reach them directly and giving them a reason to move, which an advert simply can’t do. On a market this tight, passive sourcing isn’t a nice-to-have; it’s usually the only thing that works.

Grow talent from adjacent IT

One of the most practical answers to the shortage: build, don’t just buy. A sharp network or systems engineer, or a strong IT generalist with the right instincts, can be developed into a security role with training and time — and they’ll often be more loyal for the investment. It’s slower than hiring the finished article, but on a role you genuinely can’t fill at a price you’ll pay, growing your own is frequently the sanest route.

Use contract or managed help for the gaps

You don’t always need a permanent hire for everything. A penetration test is often a project, not a full-time role. Compliance work can be brought in for a push. And plenty of smaller businesses cover day-to-day monitoring through a managed security service rather than a permanent SOC team. Match the engagement to the need — permanent for ongoing core security, contract or managed for the specialist spikes. (Our take on contract vs permanent applies squarely here.)

What to check in the interview

Beyond the obvious credentials, probe how they think about risk: a real incident they handled and what they learned, how they’d prioritise with a limited budget (a good sign — security is always about trade-offs), and whether they can explain a threat in plain English to non-technical colleagues, because half the job is getting the rest of the business to care. The ones who talk only in fear and jargon are often harder to work with than the ones who talk in practical risk.

Frequently asked questions

Why is cybersecurity talent so hard to hire in Singapore?

Demand has exploded — every business has something to protect and regulators expect it — while the supply of genuinely experienced security people hasn’t kept up. The good ones are employed, constantly approached, and rarely reading job ads, so a generic posting alone tends to fill nothing.

What cybersecurity roles do I actually need?

“Cybersecurity” is at least three different jobs: SOC / defensive (monitoring and responding to threats), penetration testing / offensive (actively finding holes), and GRC / compliance (policies, audits, regulatory alignment). Many businesses post for a hands-on hacker when what they really need first is compliance and governance. Define the actual problem before you hire.

Do cybersecurity hires need certifications?

Certifications help, especially for compliance-facing roles and getting past HR filters, but they aren’t the whole picture — a wall of acronyms isn’t the same as being able to secure your systems. Use certs as one signal, and test how the person actually reasons about risk with a real example.

Can I train IT staff into cybersecurity?

Often yes, and it’s one of the most practical answers to the shortage. A sharp network or systems engineer, or a strong IT generalist, can be developed into a security role with training and time — and tends to be more loyal for the investment. It’s slower than hiring ready-made, but frequently the sanest route on a role you can’t fill.

Should I hire cybersecurity permanently or use a managed service?

Match the engagement to the need. Permanent suits ongoing, core security; a penetration test is usually a project; compliance can be brought in for a push; and many smaller businesses cover day-to-day monitoring through a managed security service rather than a full permanent team.
