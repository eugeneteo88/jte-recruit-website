How to hire engineers in Singapore: the complete employer’s guide

The whole journey in one place — from writing the brief to making the offer to keeping them — with links to the deep-dive on each step.

Eugene Teo

Co-Founder, JTE Recruit · 14 November 2026 · 10 min read

Short answer: hiring engineers well in Singapore comes down to a handful of things done properly — and most hiring headaches trace back to skipping one of them. Define the right role (not just a title), budget the true cost, choose contract or permanent deliberately, write an ad that pulls the right people, interview for judgement even when you’re not technical yourself, make an offer they won’t reject, and — the bit everyone forgets — actually keep them once you’ve got them. This is the complete guide to that journey, and it links out to a proper deep-dive on each step. As someone who’s hired plenty of engineers myself and placed a lot more for clients, this is the map I wish every employer had before they started.

Work through it in order, or jump to the bit you’re stuck on. Either way, the throughline is simple: good engineers aren’t as scarce as it feels — most of the time the problem is somewhere in how we hire, and every part of that is fixable.

1. Start with the right brief, not the title

Nearly every bad engineering hire starts with a vague brief. “Mechanical engineer” alone covers at least five completely different jobs; “controls engineer” means something different on every factory floor. Before you write a word of an ad, get clear on what this person must actually do in their first six months. Nail that and the whole hire gets easier — the ad writes itself, the shortlist tightens, and you stop interviewing near-misses. We break down how in how to hire the right engineer.

2. Work out which engineer you actually need

“Engineer” is a family, not a job, and the discipline you pick shapes everything from where you look to what you pay. A few of the common ones, each with its own deep-dive: process engineers (who move your yield and P&L), maintenance and reliability engineers (who keep the machines running and stop them breaking), and automation and controls engineers (the tightest market right now). And do sort out the perennial project engineer vs project manager mix-up before you post — it trips up more employers than any other. Match the discipline to the real work, not to whichever title sounds most senior.

3. Budget the true cost, not just the salary

The salary is only the visible part. Employer CPF, leave, benefits and ramp-up time all add on top — and the most expensive line of all is a bad hire, or a critical role left open for months. Get honest about the full number before you post, so you can pitch an offer that actually lands. Full breakdown in what a good engineer really costs, and current ranges by role in our engineering salary guide.

4. Decide contract or permanent — on purpose

Hire permanent for ongoing, core work; hire contract for a finite project, a spike, or a specialist skill for a fixed window — and most teams actually run a blend of both. Getting this wrong is expensive in either direction. The full logic is in contract vs permanent engineers, and if the payroll admin of contract staff puts you off, contract staffing with the payroll handled takes that off your plate.

5. Write an ad that pulls the right people

If your role is getting silence or a pile of mismatches, it’s almost always the ad, not the market — a vague spec, a hidden salary, a wall of requirements, a title nobody searches for. Fix those and the same “impossible” role starts pulling people. The full checklist: why your job ad gets no good applicants.

6. Reach the ones who aren’t even applying

Here’s the hard truth about the best engineers: they’re usually employed, delivering, and not reading job ads at all. No advert reaches them. Getting to those passive candidates — and giving them a reason to consider a move they weren’t planning — is most of what a recruiter is actually for, and it’s the difference-maker on scarce roles. More on that in what a good recruiter actually does.

7. Interview for judgement, even if you’re not technical

You don’t need to test the deep engineering — you need to test what you can judge: can they explain their work in plain English, have they shipped something real, do they ask smart questions about your business, and how do they think through a problem. Being non-technical is almost an advantage here. Full playbook: how to interview an engineer when you’re not technical.

8. Weigh attitude against experience for the seat

For a lot of engineering seats — especially junior-to-mid roles with room to grow — a hungry, coachable person will overtake a jaded veteran within a year. For safety-critical, senior or regulated roles, experience is non-negotiable. Knowing which your seat needs is half the decision: attitude vs experience — which actually wins.

9. Make an offer they won’t reject

You found the right person — now don’t lose them at the final step. Most rejected offers aren’t about money; they’re about a slow process, a surprise number, no proper sell, or an unprepared candidate getting talked into staying. Close it properly: how to make an offer they won’t reject.

10. Keep them once you’ve got them

The cheapest hire is the one you don’t have to make, because your good people stay. Engineers rarely leave purely for money — it’s usually growth, the manager, or feeling taken for granted — and when someone does resign, a panic counter-offer usually backfires. The two worth reading here: why your engineers keep leaving and should you counter-offer.

11. Use the grants that help you hire and train

Singapore has real support for hiring and developing engineers — from career-conversion funding to automatic wage credits — and a lot of employers either miss it or use it wrong. Used well, it can meaningfully offset the cost of taking on someone you’ll train up rather than pay a premium for. Plain-English rundown: the employer’s guide to hiring grants.

12. Know when to get help

You can do all of this yourself — plenty of employers do, and this guide is here for exactly that. But if a role’s been open too long, if you can’t confidently judge the technical side, or if you simply don’t have the hours to reach the people who aren’t applying, that’s where we come in. Frequently asked questions

How do I hire an engineer in Singapore?

Define the actual role (not just a title), budget the true cost including CPF and ramp-up, decide contract or permanent, write a clear ad with a salary range, interview for judgement and communication, and make a well-sold offer while preparing the candidate for a counter-offer. For scarce roles, reaching passive candidates who aren’t applying is often the missing piece.

How much does it cost to hire an engineer in Singapore?

It varies widely by discipline and seniority, and the salary is only part of it — employer CPF, benefits and ramp-up add on top, and a bad hire can cost a multiple of the salary. Anchor to a current range for the specific role rather than a single headline figure; our engineering salary guide keeps ranges by level.

Should I hire a contract or permanent engineer?

Permanent for ongoing, core work; contract for a finite project, a workload spike, or a specialist skill for a set window. Many teams run a permanent core with a contract layer on top. Decide based on how long the work lasts and how central it is to the business.

How do I interview an engineer if I’m not technical?

Test what you can judge: ask them to explain a project in plain English, dig into what they personally did versus their team, give them a real problem from your business to talk through, and see whether they ask good questions about your company. Bring in a trusted technical second opinion for the deep part if you can.

Why can’t I find good engineers?

Usually one of three things: the brief is vague so you attract mismatches, the ad or salary is off-putting, or the good people simply aren’t applying because they’re employed and not looking. Fix the first two, and reach the passive ones directly for the third.

How do I keep my engineers from leaving?

Pay fairly before they have to ask, give a visible growth path, put them under a manager worth staying for, and recognise the work. Most engineers leave for growth, management or recognition rather than money alone — and a panic counter-offer after they resign rarely fixes the real cause.
