Your new hire quit in the first 90 days — here’s what went wrong

You did the hard work, found the right person — and they walked before probation ended. Here’s the real culprit, and how to fix it.

Eugene Teo

Co-Founder, JTE Recruit · 17 December 2026 · 6 min read

Here’s a gut-punch a lot of employers know: you spend weeks finding the right person, you finally hire them — and within the first three months, they’re gone. It stings, and the instinct is to blame the hire, or the market, or bad luck. But in my experience, an early quit is almost never a hiring problem. It’s an onboarding problem. The person you picked was fine; the first 90 days let them down. And the frustrating part is that it’s completely fixable, and cheap to fix. Let me walk through what actually goes wrong, and what makes a new hire stay.

Because here’s the thing — a new hire is never more likely to leave than in those first few weeks, when the excitement collides with reality and they’re quietly asking themselves “did I make a mistake?” Your job is to make the answer no.

Day one tells them everything

The number of new hires who turn up on day one to a desk that isn’t set up, no laptop, no one expecting them, a manager in back-to-back meetings — it’s astonishing, and it’s a disaster. That first day sets the tone: if it feels chaotic and like you forgot they were coming, they immediately wonder what else you’re disorganised about. It costs nothing to get this right — a ready desk, a warm welcome, someone expecting them — and it buys enormous goodwill.

They don’t actually know what they’re meant to do

A shocking number of early quits come down to this: the person was never given clarity on what success looks like. They sit there for weeks half-guessing, afraid to ask too much, feeling useless. Good people especially can’t stand feeling ineffective. Spell it out early — what does good look like in month one, who do they go to, what are the first things to get done — and give them a genuine early win so they feel capable and useful fast.

“A new hire is never more likely to leave than in those first few weeks.”

Nobody checks in — until the exit interview

The reliable way to lose a new hire is to hire them and then go quiet, assuming “no news is good news.” It isn’t. Small frustrations and doubts build up unspoken, and the first time you hear about them is when they resign. Regular, genuine check-ins in the early weeks — how’s it going, what’s confusing, what do you need — catch problems while they’re still fixable. It’s the same logic as a stay interview, just earlier.

The job wasn’t what you sold

Sometimes the culprit is the hiring itself — not the person, but the picture you painted. If the role, the pace, or the reality on the ground is meaningfully different from what they were told at interview, they feel misled, and they leave. Be honest at the offer stage about what the job actually is (we cover this in making an offer they won’t reject). A hire who joins with accurate expectations forgives a lot; one who feels sold a different job forgives nothing.

The first 90 days, done right

None of this is complicated — it’s just intentional. Make day one feel expected and organised. Give them clarity and a quick win in week one. Get them into real work with regular check-ins through the first month. And have an honest review around the three-month mark with a clear path forward. Do that, and the person you worked so hard to find actually stays — which, given what a replacement costs, is one of the highest-return things you can do as an employer.

Frequently asked questions

Why do new hires quit in the first 90 days?

Usually onboarding, not the hire itself. Common causes: a chaotic first day that signals disorganisation, no clarity on what success looks like, no check-ins so small frustrations build unspoken, or a job that turned out different from what was sold at interview. All are fixable and cheap to fix.

How do I stop new employees from leaving early?

Make day one feel expected and organised, give clear goals and an early win in the first week, check in genuinely and regularly through the first month, and be honest upfront about what the job really is. A new hire is most at risk in the first weeks, so that’s where to invest.

Is an early quit the recruiter’s fault or the employer’s?

Usually neither the recruiter nor the candidate — it’s most often the onboarding. The person picked was fine; the first 90 days let them down. Occasionally it’s a mismatch created by overselling the role at interview, which is an expectations problem to fix at the offer stage.

What should a good onboarding look like?

Intentional, not complicated: a ready desk and warm welcome on day one, clear goals and a quick win in week one, real work with regular check-ins through the first month, and an honest review at around three months with a path forward. It costs little and dramatically improves retention.

How much does it cost when a new hire quits early?

A lot — you lose the entire cost of hiring, the ramp-up time invested, and you’re back to square one, often with a demoralised team. That’s exactly why the small, cheap investment in a proper first 90 days pays for itself many times over.
