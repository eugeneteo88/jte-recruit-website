Singapore’s healthcare manpower crunch: what employers can actually do

Everyone in healthcare is fishing in the same small pool. Here’s why it’s so hard right now — and the practical things that actually move the needle.

Eugene Teo

Co-Founder, JTE Recruit · 1 December 2026 · 6 min read

Short answer: healthcare is one of the hardest sectors to staff in Singapore right now — and it’s not going to get easier soon. An ageing population is driving demand up fast, the shortage is global so you’re competing with the whole world, and burnout keeps pushing experienced people out of the field. You can’t fix the macro picture. But there’s plenty an individual employer can do — and the ones who do it well quietly staff up while everyone else complains about the market. As a recruiter who works this space, here’s what actually helps.

I’m not going to pretend there’s a magic fix. There isn’t. But there’s a real gap between the employers who struggle endlessly and the ones who cope, and it comes down to a handful of choices.

Why it’s so tight

Three forces at once. Demand is climbing as Singapore ages — more elderly care, more chronic conditions, more of everything. Supply can’t keep up, and because the shortage is worldwide, the good people have options everywhere, not just here. And the work is hard: long shifts, emotional load, and burnout that pushes experienced staff to leave healthcare altogether. So the pool isn’t just small — it’s actively leaking. Understanding that is the start, because it tells you retention matters as much as hiring.

Get the role and the expectations honest

A lot of healthcare roles sit open because the ask doesn’t match reality — the pay, the shift pattern, or the workload expected of one person. Be honest with yourself about what the role really demands, and price and describe it accordingly. In a shortage, the employer with the fair, realistic offer wins the person; the one hoping someone will tahan (endure) an unrealistic load for below-market pay keeps interviewing forever.

“In a leaking pool, every good person you keep is one you don’t have to fight the whole world to replace.”

Widen the pool sensibly

When the obvious pool is dry, look wider — carefully. Can some of the work be done by a support role (a healthcare assistant, an aide) rather than insisting on a fully-qualified professional for every task? Could you take someone with adjacent experience and develop them? Could a role be reshaped so it’s less punishing and therefore easier to fill and keep? Rethinking who can do the work, not just chasing the same scarce profile everyone else wants, opens doors.

Reach the people who aren’t applying

As in every tight market, the best healthcare people are usually employed and not scrolling job ads — they’re being looked after, or they’re quietly burnt out and open to the right conversation, but they won’t come to you through a posting. Reaching them directly, and offering something genuinely better, is often the only thing that works. That’s a big part of what a recruiter does here.

Treat retention as recruitment

Here’s the one most employers underuse: the cheapest hire is the good nurse or care worker you don’t lose. In a leaking pool, every person you keep is one you don’t have to fight the whole world to replace. Manageable workloads, feeling genuinely valued, a bit of flexibility on rostering, fair pay that keeps pace — these hold people, and they’re far cheaper than a permanent hiring war. We go deeper on that in why your care staff keep leaving.

Use flexible staffing for the gaps

You don’t always need a permanent hire for every gap. Locum and contract cover can keep you running through a shortage, a leave period, or a demand spike, without over-committing while the market’s this tight. Matching the engagement to the need — permanent for the core, flexible for the peaks — keeps you staffed and sane. (The same logic as contract vs permanent anywhere.)

Frequently asked questions

Why is healthcare so hard to staff in Singapore?

Three forces at once: an ageing population driving demand up fast, a global shortage so the good people have options everywhere, and burnout pushing experienced staff out of the field. The pool isn’t just small, it’s leaking — which is why retention matters as much as hiring.

How can employers cope with the healthcare shortage?

Get the role and pay honest and realistic, widen the pool sensibly (support roles, adjacent experience, reshaped jobs), reach the people who aren’t applying, treat retention as seriously as recruitment, and use locum or contract cover for gaps rather than leaving roles open.

Is it better to hire permanent or locum healthcare staff?

Both have a place. Permanent for your core team; locum or contract cover for shortages, leave periods and demand spikes, so you stay running without over-committing while the market is this tight. Match the engagement to the need.

How do I fill a healthcare role that’s been open for months?

Check the offer is realistic first — pay, shift pattern and workload — because unrealistic asks keep roles open. Then widen the pool (could a support role or a developed hire do it?), and reach passive candidates directly rather than relying on a job ad, which only reaches the small slice actively looking.

Does retention really help with the manpower shortage?

Yes — hugely. In a leaking pool, every good person you keep is one you don’t have to fight the whole world to replace. Manageable workloads, feeling valued, fair pay and some rostering flexibility hold people, and they’re far cheaper than a constant hiring war.
